## 1. The rival physician and antimony
**Status:** used in Page III (Rustveil), 2026-10-31. The physician is now canon (`CANON.md`, "The physician"); his role as accuser stays an open thread. Added 2026-10-26.

**The story.** A learned physician comes to town, one of the new doctors who healed with metals: antimony and mercury. He thinks Madame de Velmont is a witch; she thinks he is a poisoner.

**The rhyme.** Her glass lamp tests the truth (Dusklantern); his metal cup turns wine into poison.

**Truth of the period for the hook:**
- **"The new doctors who healed with metals"** belong to the 16th century. This is the wording for episodes.
- Reference only, never said or shown in videos: these doctors followed Paracelsus (1493–1541).
- **Antimony cups and "everlasting pills"** belong to the 17th century. Use them carefully and never present them as 1551.
- **Kohl:** a dark eye powder made from antimony or lead. The white faces and black brows of rich ladies are the same poison.

**The plant for this episode** must stand against metal. It is invented, like every plant in the series.

**Ruta's decision (2026-10-26):** Paracelsus is never named in videos. Episodes say "the new doctors who healed with metals"; his name lives only in this file, as a reference.

**For discussion. The lady's fate is open; nothing here goes into the canon yet:**
- **Option A, possible:** the physician is the one who accused Madame de Velmont when the lady fell asleep from Bride's Shadow.
- **Option B, to discuss:** the lady did not die. She slept from Bride's Shadow, and perhaps she woke in our time before the heroine did.

## 2. Real lives behind Madame de Velmont
**Status:** idea · added 2026-11-05 · not canon, only by Ruta's decision

A source for her story. **Real names are never said in videos.** Veyra may speak in general terms, e.g. *"History remembers women like her. Most of them ended in the fire."* (see the check below).

### The three women
1. **Jacqueline Felice de Almania, Paris, 1322.**
   - What happened: she healed without a licence, and the Faculty of Medicine took her to court. Eight witnesses, nearly all patients, said she cured them where licensed physicians had failed. A shopkeeper paid her only after he recovered. She was found guilty, forbidden to practise, fined, and threatened with excommunication.
   - For the series: the model for the conflict with the physician who heals with metals. Learned men against a woman who heals better.
   - Sources: Wikipedia, Hektoen International.
2. **Agnes Sampson, "the wise wife of Keith", Scotland, 1591.**
   - What happened: a poor widow, healer and midwife. She was named in a witch trial and tortured. On 28 January 1591 she was strangled and burned in Edinburgh.
   - For the series: the model for the heroine's teacher. A respected healer with no protectors.
   - Source: Wikipedia.
3. **La Voisin, Paris, 1670s.**
   - What happened: she began as a fortune-teller after her husband's ruin, then became a midwife. Her clients were the highest nobility, up to the king's mistress. She grew rich and wore a costly embroidered velvet mantle. Arrested in 1679, burned in 1680. She really did sell poisons.
   - For the series: Madame's dark mirror. The one who agreed to everything the rich asked.

### Truth of the period (verified by Ruta)
- Herbalists learned from childhood, by word of mouth, in the family and from neighbours.
- Literate women kept handwritten recipe books, passed down as family heirlooms.
- Noble ladies made remedies for their own households.
- University physicians despised herbalists.
- Poor women ran the greatest risk, especially when a cure failed.
- Historians do not know the exact share of healers among the accused.
- The myth of "millions of burned herbalists" is not supported.
- The peak of the witch hunts was the late 16th and the 17th century.

**An honest hook for the series:** *"Most healers lived and died unnoticed. But when a cure failed, the healer was the first to be blamed."*

### Episode idea: how she became a herbalist
- As a girl she is taught by a neighbour, a herbalist.
- The teacher is taken away in a cart. She is the grey-haired woman from the Page III hook. Reference image: `refs/teacher-cart-1551.jpg`.
- Perhaps the first pages of the herbarium are in the teacher's hand: the book came to the heroine as an inheritance.
- The heroine's real name is never spoken on screen.

### Check before use
- **Veyra's line "Most of them ended in the fire"** goes further than the verified facts above: historians do not know the share of healers among the accused, the "millions burned" myth is not supported, and in many places the accused were hanged, not burned. It also falls under the CANON rule that Veyra never states facts about executions unless verified. The honest hook above is safer. Ruta decides.
- **Dates:** all three women lie outside 1551 (1322, 1591, 1670s). They are a source for the story, never presented as events of her time.
